Why competition

Ambition needs somewhere to be tested.

Leadership cannot be taught in a seminar and it cannot be graded on a worksheet. It needs stakes, a deadline, teammates who are counting on you, and someone qualified watching. We use competitive engineering to manufacture exactly those conditions, at a scale a classroom cannot reach.

  • 109,000 students in 81 countries competed in FIRST Tech Challenge last season. FIRST LEGO League adds another 650,000 across 77 countries. A student is measured against a genuinely global field, not a local one.
  • 10 of those students earn the FIRST Leadership Award each season — the honor formerly called the Dean’s List, and still the highest given to an individual rather than a team.
  • 83% of FIRST alumni are pursuing a STEM major by their fourth year of college. The habit tends to hold.

We build on this framework because of how seriously it is run. The FIRST board includes a member of the executive committee of the MIT Corporation, the former deputy dean of engineering at Yale, and the president of Worcester Polytechnic Institute; its chair formerly directed NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory. More than 200 colleges, companies and associations offer scholarships specifically to FIRST students, worth over $80 million across the United States and Canada.

How we work

FIRST is the backbone. It is not the boundary.

The FIRST calendar gives us deadlines, judges and a world stage we could never build ourselves, so we use it. Around it we run our own challenges, our own events, and work that has nothing to do with robotics at all.

Competitive teams

Mentoring, inside a team

We mentor teams we founded and teams we did not — design reviews, engineering notebook coaching, judging preparation, competition strategy. We do not mentor students in a vacuum, because leadership is not visible in one. It shows up when a student has to carry a subsystem, talk down a teammate, or hold a schedule that is slipping.

Off-season challenges

Competitions we build

We design our own game challenges, with complete rule books, and run the events around them in the off-season. Students who compete in ours learn design, build and strategy directly from seasoned world-class competitors and coaches, and arrive at the regular season ready to lead it rather than survive it. Competing here also makes a student eligible for affiliate STEM programs running through the summer and into the academic year, including outreach and leadership placements.

Outside FIRST entirely

The rest of the work

Workshops for younger children at the local builder space. Sessions built around NASA-related research. Events planned with area nonprofits and STEM organizations. None of it appears on a FIRST scoresheet, and it is some of the most formative work our students do.

Programs for organizations

Standing up something that lasts

We have started teams from nothing for a decade now and handed schools and nonprofits a program they can run themselves — recruiting, coaching structure, equipment, season plan. Our students help build them, which is how they learn what running an organization actually costs.

How leadership actually grows

Give a student more to be responsible for. Repeat.

Nobody becomes a leader by being told they are one. They become one by being handed something that breaks if they drop it, and then handed something larger.

  1. On the team

    Own a subsystem. Then own the build schedule. Then be the one a struggling teammate comes to at eleven at night, three days before a qualifier.

  2. In the community

    Teach at the builder space. Start a team at a school that has none. Run an event. Raise money for STEM work in the area that has nothing to do with your own season.

  3. In the organization

    Help design the competitions other students compete in. Train the next round of mentors. Carry a piece of the thing that outlasts your own participation in it.

This is the part that separates our students, and it is also how the work compounds: every student who reaches the outer ring leaves behind more teams, more expertise and more funding in the community than they found.

What it produces

Three things a student leaves with.

The trophy is the least durable of them.

  • The skills

    CAD, code and fabrication used against a real deadline, then defended in front of judges who ask hard questions. Learned this way, they tend to stick, because they were never abstract.

  • The record

    Awards are given by name. State finalist, world championship, the Leadership Award — recognition that sits in the public record independently of anything we say about a student.

  • The reference

    A mentor who has watched a student carry a subsystem, recover from a season that fell apart, and then teach a younger team can write about them with specifics. Anyone can obtain a letter. Almost nobody can obtain one that says something only a witness would know.

For the small number of students who go furthest, our affiliate network can open doors we would not otherwise hold — selective international summer research placements among them. These sit outside a normal season and we do not promise them. They are what becomes possible once a student has earned the introduction.

Before you write to us

We are not the right fit for most families.

Expect twenty or more hours a week, year-round. It eases over the summer but it does not stop — the teams standing on a podium in spring are the ones still working in July. It is sustained across years rather than months, and it competes directly with everything else a teenager might want to do. Students who thrive here are not looking for an activity. They are looking for something hard enough to be worth the years.

We also take a small number of students and teams, because that is the number we can mentor properly. We turn teams away. If we do not think we can make a difference for your student, we would rather tell you that early than take the season.

Ask whether there is a fit.

Tell us where your student is now, what they are building toward, and how much they are actually willing to put in. We will tell you honestly — including when the answer is no.
